## 2026-08-30 — Three spec-conformance gaps a completeness audit found

**Timestamp.** 2026-08-30, after the wildlife/scout commit.

**Action.** Closed the three gaps from the tech-completeness audit that were code rather than content: the Dock counting toward Feudal, the "or 1 Castle" alternative for Imperial, and the Incas team bonus.

**Why these three and not the headline.** The audit's biggest finding was that twelve of thirty civilizations have no unique unit and no unique technology — and that one is a DATASET gap, not a code one: `units.csv` and `technologies.csv` have no rows for any of them, and §9.2 already says so. Closing it means authoring roughly 41 CSV rows, which is a different kind of work. These three are code disagreeing with a spec that is already written, which is the cheaper and more embarrassing category.

**The Dock.** §7.2's table says Dark → Feudal takes two of {Barracks, Dock, Lumber Camp, Mill, Mining Camp}. `DARK_AGE_PREREQUISITE_BUILDINGS` held four of the five, on the strength of a sentence in the same section reading "This land-only slice has no Dock". That sentence stopped being true when the naval slice shipped a Dock that §7.3 lists as a Dark Age unlock — so the spec contradicted itself and the code followed the stale half. A water opening of Dock plus Mill is an ordinary AoE2 opening and it could not age up. Both halves fixed: the set, and the sentence.

**The Castle.** §7.2 says "2 qualifying Castle Age buildings **or 1 Castle**". Only the count existed, and a Castle merely counted as one of the two, so a player who had committed to a Castle and nothing else from that tier was locked out of the age the Castle exists to reach. The alternative is the whole point — a Castle costs more than two of anything else in its tier.

**The Incas.** "Farms built 50% faster", and the only unimplemented team bonus not covered by a spec deferral: §9.2.2 names four deferred lines and this was not among them, in neither list, for several versions. The seam existed — `buildRateMultiplier`, shared by Spanish builders and Treadmill Crane — but it took only the builder's civilization, and this bonus is scoped to the BUILDING. It now takes what is under construction.

**Both tests for it, and why there are two.** A unit test on the multiplier, and a second that builds an actual farm in an actual match and compares completion ticks against a Britons control. The second exists because the audit that found this gap found it by looking for table rows whose effect nothing reads — a constant plus a unit test is exactly that shape. Red-checked by stubbing the wiring to `false`: 200 ticks against 200, and the message says the bonus never reached the builder.

**One pinned test had to change.** `prototypeRules.test.ts` asserted the Dark-Age prerequisite list as the land-only four. That pin was the shape of the defect rather than a guard against it, so it now names all five with the reason.

**Gates.** `npm test` 3,053 passed / 3 skipped, typecheck, lint, build, corpus all 0.

### Review of the above

**The fix was half-applied and the missing half was an error message.** `canAdvanceToImperialAge` learned the "or 1 Castle" rule; `researchAvailability` did not, so a Castle-Age player with one University was still told "requires 2 completed Castle Age buildings … you have 1" — advice that is now false, and reaches both the agent snapshot's `researchLocked` reason and the HUD's rejection toast. The canon says an error message must name what would satisfy it. This is exactly the class the test header in this change claimed to be guarding against — "a rule that is right in one table and never reaches the surface that consults it" — one surface further on than I looked.

**Two of my assertions were direction-only where the magnitude was free.** The end-to-end Incas test asserted `incas < britons`; review set the multiplier to 1.01 and watched it pass. The numbers were knowable — a farm is 150 build ticks plus a ~50-tick walk both civs share, so 150/1.5 gives Incas 150 against Britons' 200 — and are now asserted exactly. The new message test was worse: it asserted the reason contained "castle", and the candidate list already ends "…, monastery, or castle", so it passed with the alternative removed. It now asserts the alternative phrase, red-checked.

**Two controls that could not fail.** The Dark-Age exclusion list named houses, farms, walls and the Town Center but not `outpost` or `fish-trap` — the two Dark-Age-buildable non-qualifying buildings, which are precisely what a widened set would sweep up. And the imperial refusal control passed under any implementation that always returned false, so it now carries the positive case alongside it.

**One cost I added on a hot path.** `teamHasCivilization` scans the civilization map and calls `areAllied`; I evaluated it for every builder on every tick regardless of what was being built, one commit after taking tick cost from 15.13 to 5.45 ms/tick. Short-circuited on the building type.

**And a value I took without checking.** `civilizations.csv` says 50%; DE raised it to 100% and later replaced the bonus entirely. The CSV is this repo's stated authority so the number stands, but every neighbouring bonus in §9.2 carries a sourcing note and mine had none — and there is a live lesson in this repo saying a game-rule claim is a hypothesis to check against the wiki. The divergence is now recorded in both the constant and the spec.

**Corrections to documents.** §7.2's other half still said the qualifying sets are "the non-Town-Center buildings introduced in the prior age", which sweeps in walls, gates and towers and had been flagged as wrong by a reviewer on 2026-06-17; a §7.2 conformance pass that rewrote the sentence beside it and left that is the same class it set out to close, so both sets are now named explicitly. A live lesson still described the Dock as "absent in this land-only slice". Two spec citations pointed at §11.11 instead of §9.2.2.

**On CI.** The Windows job went red on the previous commit: `selectionActivity` timed out at 30s. It takes 8s here, and every machine that runs it in CI is slower — the budget had roughly no headroom, and the new tests in that commit competing for cores was enough to cross it. Fixed on both sides: the marginal test has a real budget with the measurement written next to it, and my own tests were re-measured and shortened where the signal allowed (the corner floors run 400 ticks instead of 1,200; the retaliation horizon 300 instead of 1,200, since the wolf is at 1/25 by tick 200). The discriminating corner run keeps its full 1,200 ticks because its signal does not exist below that — 17 cells against the broken build's 19 at 600, with the gap only opening later. An A/B on the scout fix itself put its cost at ~2% of two heavy AI suites, so it is not the cause.

### The behind-building silhouette: attempted, measured, reverted

The cue is opaque and reads as a unit standing ON the building. I made it translucent — its own `occlusion` material, unlit like `ui` but transparent, with the `ui` lane left opaque so the selection outline and drag rectangle stay crisp. The showcase capture looked right, the pixel diff was 0.09% and confined to the silhouette, and the real-map capture showed the wall and window reading through the shape.

Then review measured it properly and it does not survive.

It is close to a no-op under the art style the game ships. The default is `moebius`, and its tone-band quantiser snaps 24.1% of the cue's pixels byte-identical to the opaque render. Every screenshot I judged this by was `painted`.

It introduces seams. A translucent instanced part that overlaps itself blends twice, and the mirror copies a unit's own parts — belt inside tunic, apron coplanar with the tunic face, hair over the crown, arms rolled into shoulders. Solved per-pixel: 452 px at the declared 0.55, 68 at 0.7975 (1−0.45²), 6 at 0.9089 (1−0.45³). Fourteen percent of the body drew at the wrong opacity.

And it broke the half of the cue that matters most. The enemy colour is a coral in the same hue family as every roof in the palette; its mean distance from the background over a bright roof fell from 204.2 to 82.0, while the friendly blue — orange's complement — held. No fixture spawns an enemy-owned occluded unit, so every capture in my evidence set was the blue one. The cue exists to answer "are those mine?", and I degraded the *not mine* answer without seeing it.

Reverted. The engine has the cure for the seams — `markSingleLayerTransparencyInternal`, whose own header describes this exact artefact — and does not export it from `voxel/three`; that is now in `docs/engine-feedback/current.md` as an ask, per the report-don't-modify-engine rule. The defect is registered OPEN with the measurement method the next attempt should use, because the thing that actually went wrong here was not the technique, it was judging a rendered image from one capture, of one art style, with one owner, and calling it verified.

Review also caught a third stale metrics pin I had missed — `voxel-terrain-water.spec.ts` carried the same `materialResources: 7 / instanceBatches: 9` as the two I updated, so the browser suite was red at 1 failed / 134 passed while I had read a piped `tail` status as green. That is the piping lesson again, in a suite I had already been warned about.

### AI self-play audit: what a 40-minute match actually exercises

The standing goal asks for feature completeness "after verifying with AI self-play", and I had never run that audit — the corpus checks age progression and the profiler measures tick cost, but nothing asked what a full match actually touches. So: `aoe2-prototype`, both seats AI, 24,000 ticks (40 minutes of game time), sampling every 250.

    ages first reached   dark 250/250 · feudal 9,250 (owner 2) / 12,500 (owner 1) · castle NEVER
    unit types seen (4)  militia · scout · spearman · villager
    building types (8)   barracks · farm · house · lumber-camp · mill · mining-camp · town-center · watch-tower
    population           t=8,000  11 v 12   →   t=16,000  15 v 29   →   t=24,000  3 v 25
    net losses           71

Two things follow, and the second is the finding.

The match RESOLVES, which is the good news: owner 2 out-economies owner 1 from about tick 12,000 and then destroys it — population 15 down to 3 while the winner holds at 25, with 71 net losses. Combat, targeting, production and the age race all work end to end without a harness propping them up.

But **neither player leaves the Feudal Age in forty minutes**, and the roster the whole match exercises is four unit types and eight building types. No Archery Range, Stable, Blacksmith, Market, Monastery, University or Castle; no archer, no knight, no siege, no monk, no ship. Against a content audit that puts 137 of 138 technologies live with real effects, 93 units trainable and 25 buildings buildable, self-play exercises a small fraction of the game. That is not a claim that the rest is broken — the fixtures and the browser suite reach it — but it is a claim that self-play does NOT currently verify feature completeness, which is what the goal asks it to do. The AI's build order stops at the Dark-Age four (barracks, mill, lumber camp, mining camp) and nothing drives it into the Feudal buildings that gate everything above them.

Recorded rather than fixed: making the AI play a full-length game through Castle and Imperial is its own piece of work, and it is now the largest single gap between "the game has these features" and "playing it exercises them".

One methodological note, because it nearly cost the finding: my first pass read `getEconomyState().playerAges`, which does not exist — the field is `ages` — and printed `undefined` for every sample, which I could easily have read as "the AI never ages up at all". The field name was wrong, not the game. Printing the object rather than a derived boolean is what made it obvious.

### Why AI self-play never leaves Feudal — measured, two fixes tried and reverted

Following the audit above, I went after the largest gap: self-play exercises four unit types and eight buildings against a nearly complete content set, because no match ever reaches Castle Age.

The chain is now measured rather than guessed. On `default-seed` the winning AI ends the match with **food 1571, gold 1140, stone 750 and wood 38, holding no Feudal buildings at all**. Castle needs 800 food and 200 gold — it has both, several times over — plus two qualifying Feudal buildings, and those cost 150–175 wood each. Wood never exceeds ~50 on any seed, either side, at any sample.

I tried the two obvious fixes and neither worked, which is worth more than the attempts:

*More wood villagers* — Feudal split `{food:7, wood:4, …}` to `{food:7, wood:6, …}`, roughly 7 wood villagers to 9. No seed reached Castle; variety unchanged. So wood INCOME is not the constraint.

*Unblocking the farm gate* — `pickNextBuildTarget` returns `farm` on essentially every decision tick, so I held farms to the floor while the age-up prerequisites were missing. It made `aoe2-prototype` slightly worse (eight building types to seven) and changed nothing elsewhere. The reason is instructive: a farm is CONSUMED, so the owned count falls back below even a floor of two and the branch re-fires regardless of what the floor is.

What is left is wood SPEND. Every point goes to houses for a growing population and to replacing eaten farms, and the first 150-wood Blacksmith never gets funded. And the age-up reserve makes the state stable rather than transient: `ageUpReserveCost` is empty until the owner already QUALIFIES, so an AI that cannot qualify has nothing protecting a stockpile it also cannot spend — food and gold pile up unspendably while the one resource that would unblock it sits at zero. Forty minutes changes nothing because nothing in the loop pushes back.

Both experiments are reverted; the tree is unchanged. The defect is registered with the acceptance metric a fix must use — what a match EXERCISES across at least three seeds, not Feudal timing, which both failed hypotheses left untouched while changing nothing that matters.

### A fourth attempt at the Feudal wall, and an instrument flaw

The asymmetry looked like the answer: `ageUpReserveCost` protects the age-up RESEARCH, but only once the owner already qualifies, so nothing protects the cost of BECOMING qualified. The AI defends a purchase it cannot yet make and spends the money for the one it can. So I made the farm wait unless the wood also covered the next qualifying building — six unit tests including a red-check, wired through the caller so it was not a table nothing reads.

Measured across the three seeds: `aoe2-prototype` gained a Blacksmith, `default-seed` lost a building, `corpus-seed-b` lost its farms entirely. Reserving for every still-needed prerequisite instead of just the next one moved `default-seed` to ten building types and cost the other two seeds their farms. No seed reached Castle under either variant. Starving the food economy to fund a hall is not a trade worth making. Reverted, tests included.

Four hypotheses now, four failures, each measured: more wood villagers, relaxing the farm gate, reserving for the next prerequisite, reserving for all of them. The consistent shape is that every lever I have pulled moves wood between two things that both need it, and none of them creates any.

And I nearly drew a false conclusion from my own instrument. The exercise metric counts distinct building types across the MATCH, which unions both owners — so a run reporting a Blacksmith and an Archery Range does not show that either owner had two. I had started writing "it qualifies and still will not advance, so the blocker is elsewhere", which that measurement cannot support. Anything about qualification has to be per-owner. The union metric is still right for what it claims and understates the problem if anything, but it is not the metric for the next question, and the next attempt should build the per-owner one first.

### The Feudal wall, root-caused

Four hypotheses had failed, which is two past the point where this repo's canon says to stop guessing and search deliberately. So I stopped proposing fixes and instrumented the decision itself. On `aoe2-prototype`, owner 2, 20,000 ticks:

    next=farm  ongoing=0  max=21  wonder=false  vills=22  builder=2162   x164
    WHY farm   builder=2162  anchor=SOME  afford=FALSE  wood=0..24  cost={wood:60}

The AI decides, has a builder, has capacity, has a valid anchor, and cannot pay — 164 times. Every previous hypothesis had been about which building it should *choose*, and the choice was never the problem.

Tallying what it did manage to spend: 715 wood on six buildings, and **525 wood on 21 spearmen**. 1,240 wood in 2,000 seconds is 0.62/s against a nominal 2.73/s for seven gatherers — 23% efficiency, the rest walking. Military takes 42% of what arrives.

And it was designed in by a comment that expired. `aiSystemProductionPhase` says *"Military is the only pre-age-up food/gold spend (builds spend wood/stone)"* and the age-up reserve is built on that split. A spearman costs 25 wood. So military and construction compete for the one scarce resource with nothing arbitrating, the 150 for a Blacksmith never accumulates, the AI never qualifies — and because `ageUpReserveCost` is empty until it qualifies, it also banks food and gold it cannot spend. That is the stable state: 980–1,922 food at the end of a match, and wood that never clears 24.

Adding the next qualifying building's wood to that same reserve — the pattern the file already implements for the age-up — put `default-seed` owner 2 into the **Castle Age at tick 21,500**, the only time any player has reached it here, with unit variety up from four types to five. Not adopted: it costs a unit type on the boot map and on `corpus-seed-b`, which is a regression by this repo's own adoption rule, and gating it behind an established economy restores the military everywhere and loses the Castle everywhere. The reserve has to bite early to work. That is a tuning problem with a known mechanism instead of an unknown, which is the actual deliverable here.

One more thing fell out of the long-horizon run: at 60,000 ticks every number is byte-identical to 24,000. `default-seed` owner 1 ends with zero units, a Town Center it cannot use, and 43 food against a villager's 50 — permanently unable to act — and `getMatchState().outcome` is still `running`. A decided match runs forever because a player who can neither produce nor gather is not defeated.

### The seventh experiment: the obvious pairing, and why it is wrong

If the wood reserve costs military because the spearman is the AI's only Feudal unit and it costs 25 wood, then the fix looks obvious: give it a wood-free alternative. Militia costs 60 food and 20 gold, and this AI banks both in the hundreds. Add it to the Feudal mix, keep the reserve, and the army should grow out of the surplus while the wood accumulates.

Measured: peak military HALVED — 8 to 4 on two seeds — and the Castle Age was lost. Militia's 60 food buys fewer units than the spearman's 35, so trading a wood cost for a food cost bought a smaller army AND spent the food the age-up needs. The pairing that reads well off the cost table is wrong the moment the food side is measured.

Seven experiments now. The scorecard for the one that works, reserve-alone, per owner across three seeds: `default-seed` clearly better — owner 2 qualifies at 20,000, reaches Castle at 21,500, building types 7 to 9, unit types 4 to 5. `aoe2-prototype` mixed — owner 1 gains two building types and loses a unit type, owner 2 loses a building type. `corpus-seed-b` slightly worse. One large win, two small losses, and a loss on the boot map is precisely what this repo's adoption rule refuses, having withdrawn an economy tuning on that exact ground before.

So it stays unadopted and the remaining work is named: sweep the reserve's SIZE and DURATION rather than fire another single-shot experiment at it. The mechanism is no longer in question — only how hard to press it.

### The AI reaches the Castle Age (v0.3.169)

The sweep the last entry called for, run properly, and it lands.

Two knobs, three seeds, the metric being what a match EXERCISES rather than how fast Feudal arrives. Reserve sizes 0/60/75/150/175 against Feudal wood weights 4/5/6/7/8, plus a second stop condition (reserve until the first qualifying building, or until the second).

    reserve 150, wood 4   boot map: no castle       default-seed: castle 21,500, peak military HALVED
    reserve 0,   wood 6   boot map: no castle       income alone — the spearman ate each 25 as it landed
    reserve 150, wood 6   BOOT MAP: castle 20,500   default-seed: castle 22,750
                          peak military 8 -> 9      buildings 7 -> 12, units 4 -> 7
                          units 4 -> 6

Neither half works alone and both red-check independently against the end-to-end test — revert either and it returns "no player reached the Castle Age". That is the part worth keeping: I had tried each lever separately across four earlier experiments and concluded from each failure that the lever was wrong. It was the pairing that was missing. More wood income does nothing while the spearman eats every 25 as it lands; the reserve alone buys buildings by halving the army; together the reserve directs the extra income to the buildings and the army grows anyway.

150 and 175 are identical because the reserve clamps to the next building's cost and the Blacksmith is 150. Wood 6 gives the earliest boot-map Castle of the family; 7 gives one more unit type there and a later Castle.

**The cost, and it is real.** `corpus-seed-b` regresses — both owners lose a unit type and about half their peak military, and neither reaches Castle. The boot map's losing player drops a unit type too. That does not strictly satisfy the no-regression rule this repo used to withdraw a Dark-Age tuning, and I am adopting it anyway on a stated ground: that rule's metric was Feudal timing, and this one's is what the match exercises. The game now reaches an age it had never reached, on the map players actually boot, with a bigger army than before. Recorded as a judgement so it can be reversed on evidence.

**One test had to move and it is not a weakened guard.** `aiFeudalStone` pins that a Feudal AI banks a Watch Tower's 125 stone from zero. Raising wood's weight takes stone's share of a fixed cap from 1/13 to 1/15 — 13% fewer stone villagers — and moved the crossing from ~8,300 ticks to 9,500. Measured over 20,000 ticks on that fixture: peak stone 220. The property holds and only the timing moved, so the horizon went from 18 to 22 blocks with the measurement written beside it; that file's own header records doing exactly this when the movement clock moved the same crossing.

**And a comment that had become false.** `aiSystemProductionPhase` read "military is the only pre-age-up food/gold spend (builds spend wood/stone)". A spearman costs 25 wood. The whole defect sat behind that sentence: the age-up reserve was built on a split the roster had already broken, so nothing arbitrated between soldiers and buildings for the scarcest resource. It now says what is true, next to the reserve that fixes it.

#### Review of the Castle-Age change, and the floor it was missing

The critic ran 40+ real matches across 11 seeds and found the reserve had no defensive floor. Across those seeds it cost **21% of all peak military**, and on every seed whose peak wood never reached the reserve it blocked **100%** of military training for the whole age while still never affording the building — pure loss, army pinned at 3 against a baseline of 6-7. It also found my "inseparable pair" claim was boot-map-specific (reserve-alone reaches Castle on `default-seed` 1,250 ticks EARLIER, and on `black-forest` for both owners), that wood 6 is a local optimum on a jagged objective where 5/8/9 qualify more owner-slots, that the reserve re-derived qualification instead of using the predicate three lines above it (wrong for the Khmer), priced at the undiscounted cost (wrong for the Malians), counted foundations, and kept a fourth copy of the qualifying-building list. Plus three wrong numbers in my own docs.

The floor it suggested — `attackGroupSize`, 5 — turns out to make the reserve a **no-op**: `militaryGrowthPausedForAgeUp` already stops growth at 5, so the wood drain this reserve exists to stop happens entirely below it, in replacing losses. Measured at 5: zero Castle Ages, byte-identical to baseline. So I swept the floor: 0/2/3/4/5 over three seeds.

    floor 0   2 castles   50 bldg   25 unit   military 32   (the reviewed version)
    floor 2   2 castles   51        27        military 31
    floor 3   1 castle    50        26        military 37   <- adopted
    floor 4   1 castle    48        27        military 35
    floor 5   0 castles   44        24        military 37   (= baseline)

**3 costs nothing.** Peak military identical to baseline, six more building types, two more unit types, and a Castle Age baseline never reached. The boot map does not regress on any metric — which is what the no-regression rule I was about to override actually asks for, so there is no longer a judgement call to defend.

The headline changed with it, and the docs now say what ships: the boot map becomes ELIGIBLE for the Castle Age at 23,500 having never qualified at all, and `default-seed` reaches the age itself at 24,000. The other five findings are fixed — the predicate, the discounted price, completed-only counting, and the list now DERIVED from `agePrerequisiteBuildingTypes` rather than restated a fourth time. The identity assertion in the unit test is gone; every case is a branch now.

### A decided match: fix attempted, reproduced-broken by review, reverted

The register carried "a decided match never resolves": on `default-seed` an AI finished a 40-minute match with zero units, five buildings including a Town Center, and 43 food against a villager's 50 — unable to gather, unable to train, unable ever to do either — while `getMatchState().outcome` still read `running`.

Conquest is not the gap; its rule is AoE2's own and a Town Center was standing. So I built resignation: an owner with no units that cannot afford the cheapest unit any completed production building it owns can train is eliminated, behind a grace period. Forcing fixture, control fixture, red-check on both, all six gates green.

Review broke it three ways, two of them reproduced.

**It declares a living player dead.** `enqueueTraining` debits the stockpile at ENQUEUE and creates the unit entity 250 ticks later. A player that has just correctly spent its food on eight villagers therefore has zero food and zero units — and my rule resigns it, with eight paid-for villagers in its queue. The reviewer produced a match ending "All enemy forces have been eliminated" against a standing Town Center. Against a human that is a false DEFEAT one tick before a villager pops, and the pop-blocked branch makes the window unbounded.

**Three unit-free income routes make the premise false.** A Market converts wood to gold to food with no unit anywhere. Relic gold pays a per-tick trickle. Ally tribute requires nothing of the recipient but a resource bank. Each is a live player my rule judged dead. I also priced with `trainingCost` where the charge is `effectiveTrainingCost` — a Hindustanis villager costs 46, so a player with 46-49 food and a Town Center can train right now and was resigned.

**And the grace period rested on a false premise of mine.** I wrote that the longest fixture horizon was 8,000 ticks and set the grace to 12,000. It is not: `aiReachesCastleAge` runs 24,000 — a test I wrote the same day, whose own comment says "half a real 24,000-tick match", so the number was already in front of me — plus `aiFeudalStone` and `gatherDomain` at 14,000, a farm test at 21,600, the CI corpus at 13,100 and `npm run playtest` at 30,000. Because `step()` no-ops once an outcome is set, the break would have been SILENT: the world freezes and assertions read frozen state rather than failing. Two dev scripts, including `ai:tickrate` — the tool I used for this session's tick-cost work — loop on `world.tick` with no outcome check and hang forever.

**Reverted, and not because the holes are individually unfixable.** Each is. The shape is the problem: proving a position TERMINAL means enumerating every route by which resources can still arrive, and that enumeration rots silently as the game gains features. It would have been correct the day it shipped and wrong the next time someone added a trickle. A future attempt should look for a POSITIVE signal that a player is still doing something rather than argue from absence — the queue, the market, the trickle and the tribute were all things this rule had to know about only because it was arguing the negative.

**The symptom had already gone anyway, which is the finding worth keeping.** Re-measured after v0.3.169, at 30,000 ticks:

    default-seed    o1  24 units / 17 buildings      o2  50 units / 28 buildings
    aoe2-prototype  o1  26 units / 11 buildings      o2  40 units / 17 buildings

Before that change `default-seed` owner 1 ended with ZERO units and five buildings. The economy fix did not only get a player to the Castle Age; it stopped the loser being wiped out at all, and both sides now field real armies deep into the match. The never-resolving match was a downstream symptom of the economy collapse — so the cost of getting resignation wrong is a stolen game, and the benefit is currently theoretical. That is the trade that decided the revert.

Four fixture edits went with it. I had changed the decorative opponent in four vision/aggro fixtures from a house to a Town Center with 200 food, each with a comment claiming the change was needed. The reviewer reverted all four and ran the suites: 9 of 9 pass. They were leftovers from an abandoned 2,400-tick grace, and they had altered the opponent's vision radius in the file whose subject is vision.

**One real fix did come out of it, and it was already live.** `scripts/aiTickRate.mjs` and `scripts/aiTrajectory.mjs` loop on `while (world.tick - start < maxTicks) { step(...) }` with no outcome check, and `step` returns WITHOUT advancing once a match has an outcome — so either script spins forever the moment one side is wiped out. That needs no resignation rule: conquest resolves matches today. Red-checked both ways on `conquest-victory-fixture`, which resolves at tick 117: with the guard the script prints "match ended (victory) at tick 117 of 5000 — stopping early" and exits 0; without it, killed at 40 seconds (exit 124). `runPlaytest` has had this check all along, which is why nobody noticed the two scripts lacked it. `ai:tickrate` is the tool this session used for its tick-cost measurements.

### Tech completeness: the gap measured properly, and the first three closed

The standing goal names "tech" as one of its dimensions, and an earlier audit in this session had put the gap at "~41 unwritten CSV rows for 12 civs". That framing was wrong in a way worth recording: the CSV is COMPLETE — all 30 civilizations carry a `unique_unit` and a `unique_tech`. The gap is in the CODE.

Measuring it took four attempts, and three of them lied. A grep restricted to `types.ts` reported the Jaguar Warrior missing, which is false — it is in 20 files. Widening to the whole tree via `subprocess` reported it missing again, and the cause was Windows stripping the single quotes from the grep pattern, so `'[a-z0-9-]+'` became `[a-z0-9-]+` and matched bare word fragments — the token set was full of things like `olves` (from "Wolves"). Only after extracting the tokens in Python and **validating the instrument against two known-present and two known-absent names** did the measurement hold up.

The real gap: all 19 original civilizations are complete. The 12 expansion civilizations — Berbers, Burmese, Ethiopians, Incas, Indians, Italians, Khmer, Magyars, Malians, Portuguese, Slavs, Vietnamese — have **17 unique units and 22 unique technologies**, none implemented.

Three are now closed, and the values were verified against the wiki rather than written from memory, per the standing lesson. Memory would have been wrong twice: **Shatagni gives +2 range, not +1**, and the Incas' Couriers now grants armour where it originally gave +10% speed. Also learned in passing: the Slavs' **Orthodoxy was REMOVED in Dynasties of India** and replaced by Detinets — the same CSV-versus-live-DE divergence as the Incas farm bonus, and a reason any future row here needs the same check.

    Shatagni      Indians  Imperial  500f 650g  Hand Cannoneers +2 range
    Recurve Bow   Magyars  Imperial  600w 400g  Cavalry Archers +1 range, +1 attack
    Farimba       Malians  Imperial  650f 400g  Cavalry +5 attack

Farimba's real scope is Scout Cavalry, Light Cavalry, Knight, Cavalier, Camel Rider and Heavy Camel Rider; this roster has no camels and no separate Scout Cavalry, so the set is narrower — deliberately, and said so in the code rather than left to look like an omission.

**Five registration points, and the typechecker found the one I missed.** A unique technology needs an entry in the type union, in `RESEARCH_COSTS`, in the research-time table, in the Castle's `RESEARCHES_BY_BUILDING` list, and in the HUD display-name formatter — which is an exhaustive switch, so `tsc` refused the build until it had all three. The spec's own §9.2.2 table is checked against `UNIQUE_TECHNOLOGIES` by an architecture test, which failed until the table and its count sentence were updated; that guard's regex hardcoded "N of the nineteen", nineteen being the ORIGINAL civilizations' count, so it had to learn that the denominator moved. Its number-word table also read "Twenty-two" as "two" until the pattern allowed hyphens.

The nineteen remaining technologies are not all reachable with the current effect vocabulary — Maghrabi Camels needs regeneration, Druzhina needs splash, Silk Road and Paper Money need economy seams, and several (Couriers, Tusk Swords, Chatras, Royal Heirs, Mercenaries) depend on unique units that do not exist yet. Those are the next slice, and the ones needing new seams should be taken with the seam rather than bent into the existing one.

**Kasbah too, and the remaining gap is now a checked ledger.** Kasbah (Berbers, Castle, 250 food + 250 gold, 40 s) makes Castles work 25% faster. In AoE2 it helps the whole TEAM; `uniqueTechTrainTimeMultiplier` reads one owner's researched set, so here it helps the researching player — identical in a 1v1, narrower in a team game, and said so in the code rather than left looking like the full bonus. Widening it means teaching that seam about `teamHasCivilization`, which `civBuildingBonuses` already does.

The more durable piece is `tests/architecture/expansionCivContentGap.test.ts`, which turns "we know there is a gap" into a list that fails when it drifts. It asserts the missing set EQUALS the ledger, so closing an entry forces a visible edit and nothing can silently fall out of the implemented set either.

It caught its own author on the first run. My ledger listed Howdah and Pavise as missing; they are not — except they are. A text scan over every single-quoted identifier in `src` found both, because a **howdah is the platform on an elephant and a pavise is a shield**: two voxel render PART names colliding with two technology names. The scan could not tell content from geometry. The test now asks the authoritative registries instead — `RESEARCH_COSTS` for technologies, `TRAINABLE_UNITS_BY_BUILDING` for units — because what makes a technology real is a cost the validator can charge, and what makes a unit real is a building that can train it. Both false positives are pinned as explicit assertions so the text-scan approach cannot come back.

That is four wrong measurements of the same quantity in one sitting: scoped to one file; twice through a grep whose quotes Windows stripped; and a whole-source text scan that could not distinguish a technology from a shield. The instrument-validation assertion in that test exists because of them.

**And the fog-memory entry's prediction paid off within the day.** It closed with "every remaining `advanceTicks(n)`-then-assert in the browser suite is the same defect waiting for a different scheduler". The combat spec that aims at a MOVING enemy failed twice in the full suite this session and passed 4/4 in isolation — I had written the first one off as contention. It is not: the simulation was unpaused, so the view's frame loop advances the world in real time and extra ticks pass between reading the target's position and clicking beside it, as many as the machine's load allows. Its 4-attempt retry loop was compensating for the wall-clock dependency instead of removing it. `setPaused(true)` removes it, and the suite is 135/135.

The rule sharpens to: a browser assertion is load-dependent whenever the thing it measures can move without the test asking. Only **4 of 34** browser specs pause — not thirty defects, since most assert nothing that moves, but a counted population rather than a surprise.

### The self-play acceptance test becomes a command

The standing goal makes AI self-play the acceptance test for feature completeness, and it had been run ad hoc perhaps a dozen times this session — each run rebuilding the metric from scratch. One of those rebuilds silently unioned both owners, so it could not show whether any single player had qualified for an age, and I nearly drew a conclusion from it.

`npm run ai:selfplay-audit` now owns that measurement. It reports PER OWNER, never merged, because a union across players answers a different question and flatters the result. Current state, three seeds, 24,000 ticks:

    seed              own  feudal  qualified  castle  imperial  bldgs  units  army
    aoe2-prototype    1    12500   -          -       -         6      4      4
    aoe2-prototype    2    9250    23500      -       -         9      5      8
    default-seed      1    14000   19750      -       -         10     4      6
    default-seed      2    9750    18250      24000   -         12     5      8
    corpus-seed-b     1    8750    -          -       -         7      4      5
    corpus-seed-b     2    12250   -          -       -         6      4      6

    6 owner-slots: 1 reached Castle, 0 reached Imperial.

That is the honest position, and writing it down as a command rather than a paragraph is the point: the next person to ask "does a game of this reach the content the game has?" gets the same metric rather than a fresh one. `corpus-seed-b` is the visible weak spot — neither owner qualifies there, which is the regression the Castle-Age work knowingly accepted and recorded.

### corpus-seed-b, and two more negatives

The new audit command named `corpus-seed-b` as the weak seed — neither owner qualifies there — so I measured it.

Owner 1 finishes with **food 1,895, gold 790, stone 440 and wood 20**: the original defect's shape, one seed further on. Cumulative income over 20,000 ticks is food 3,189, wood **1,300**, gold 760, stone 380. Every point of that wood is spent as it arrives; the balance never approaches the 150 a Blacksmith costs. And of seven villagers assigned to wood, **two are gathering at any moment** — three walking out, two walking back, about 25% of nominal.

That pointed at a real divergence from AoE2: the AI builds **exactly one lumber camp and one mining camp for the whole game**. `missing(type)` means "owns none", and the camp branch lives inside the Dark-Age arm of `pickNextBuildTarget`, so past that age it is unreachable regardless. A DE player builds a camp per woodline as the near trees go.

Closing it does not help, which is worth as much as the finding. Allowing a second camp changed nothing at all — three thresholds, byte-identical results — because the AI cannot afford the 100 wood. Allowing camps past the Dark Age as well moved one seed by a single building type, and no seed reached the Castle Age. Both reverted.

Eight measured negatives on this wall now. The pattern across all of them is the same: every lever moves wood between things that all need it, and none creates any. The next candidates have to be on the GATHER side — walk distance, camp siting, gatherers per drop-off — and the number to beat is 25% of nominal, not an age timing. Age timings have moved under interventions that changed nothing real, which is exactly why the audit measures what a match EXERCISES instead.

### Where the 25% goes: the approach, not the haul

Having said the next number to beat was 25% of nominal gather rate, I went and found where it goes. 459 samples over 20,000 ticks on `corpus-seed-b`, both halves of a gather trip in tiles:

    villager -> target    median 6    p90 20    max 61
    target   -> dropoff   median 6    p90 9     max 50

The haul is healthy, and the arithmetic says so: carry is 10, wood gathers at 0.385/s, so a full load is ~26 seconds of chopping against a median 6-tile haul — 7.5 seconds each way at 0.8 tiles/s. That predicts about 68% efficiency. The observed 25% is not the haul's doing.

It is the APPROACH, and specifically its tail. At the 90th percentile a villager walks 20 tiles to reach its tree — 25 seconds, as long as chopping the load it went for — and the worst case is 61 tiles, 76 seconds. The median approach is unremarkable; one trip in ten costs more walking than it returns in wood.

That also rewrites what the failed camp experiments meant. A villager takes the nearest AVAILABLE tree, so once the near woodline is worked out the nearest is far, and the AI cannot answer the way a player does — plant a camp at the new woodline — because it owns exactly one for the whole game and cannot afford a second. The camp experiments did not disprove the fix; they showed it gated behind affording 100 wood, which is the same wall from the other side. The approach tail is the cost; the camp is the instrument for cutting it; the wood to build it is what is missing.

So the next attempt has a target that is neither an age timing nor a resource weight: cut the p90 approach from 20 tiles. Everything else measured this session has been downstream of it.

### The tenth attempt: a win that was made of its own bugs

The diagnosis said cut the approach tail by planting a camp at the receding woodline, and the tenth attempt appeared to do exactly that. A second Lumber Camp, with `dropOffAnchorFor` skipping resources an existing camp already serves, took three seeds from 1 Castle-Age owner-slot to 3 — building types 50 to 55, unit types 26 to 31, boot map not regressed, all six gates green, and the implementation reproduced the sweep to the tick.

Review ran twelve seeds against my three and found two defects.

A null anchor fell back to the Town Center. Null used to mean only "nothing in range"; with the filter it also means "everything is already served", and a Lumber Camp on the Town Center shortens no carry at all — `canDropOffAt` already accepts wood there. That is 100 wood of the scarcest resource in this economy, for nothing.

And the second Lumber Camp displaced the Mining Camp. Both cost 100 wood, lumber is asked first, and the Dark-Age arm is the only place either is reachable. Over twelve seeds, mining camps fell 17 to 15 and the owner-slots ending with no gold or stone drop-off at all rose 7 to 9 — permanently.

**Fixing both destroyed the benefit.** Ten seeds, twenty owner-slots, with the null case refusing to build and the opening order putting the first of each drop-off ahead of any second:

    camps   Castle   building types   unit types   peak army
    1           4         156              89          110
    2           4         160              85           99
    3           2         159              83           98

No Castle-Age benefit, and it costs unit variety and a tenth of the peak army. The three-seed win was not the mechanism; it was the two bugs. Reverted in full, tests included.

**The accident points somewhere real, though.** Displacing the Mining Camp helped because it spends 100 wood on wood instead of on gold and stone — and this AI banks gold and stone unspent for the whole game, measured over and over: 790 gold and 440 stone against 20 wood at the end of a match. So the next hypothesis is not another camp. It is that the Mining Camp is a poor buy on these maps, and the AI should not build one while the resources it serves pile up unused. Cheap to test, and it exists only because fixing a bug made a result worse.

Three other things review was right about, all mine. My approach figures do not reproduce — the claimed median 5 to 10-11 and p90 9 to 23 measure as median 9 to 10 and p90 12 to 13, and the only variant producing my p90 is a TICK-WEIGHTED sample, which is length-biased by construction since a long walk contributes one sample per tick. I never committed the instrument, so those published numbers were unreproducible by anyone including me. "Allowing a second gained nothing at first" is false at scale: the camp count alone gives 7 Castle slots against a baseline 5, and the filter adds one. And the mechanism I wrote into two source comments is not the one implemented — camps only build inside the Dark-Age arm, measured at ticks 5,900 / 7,550 / 10,275, and `DROP_OFF_ANCHOR_RADIUS` bounds the anchor to 12 tiles of the Town Center, so it can never reach a woodline at the distance I described.

All three of my tests were theatre, proven by mutation: setting the served radius to 1 passes them, and setting it to 1000 — which would plant every second camp on the Town Center, the D1 defect in every game — passes them too.

### The twelfth attempt, and the horizon that judged all twelve

The eleventh attempt's accident said the Mining Camp is a poor buy while gold and stone bank unspent. So the twelfth moved it out of the Dark Age into the Feudal arm, placed after the age-up qualifying halls on the principle that aging up outranks shortening a carry. Ten seeds: Castle-Age slots 4 to 6, unit types 89 to 92, peak army flat, and the boot map reaching the age at tick 22,250 where HEAD reached it never. All six gates green. The best-measuring, best-reasoned attempt of the twelve.

Review killed it three times over.

**It is the never-build variant wearing a different name.** The insertion point sits after a loop that demands blacksmith AND archery-range AND stable AND market, and the AI qualifies for the Castle Age with two of those and rarely finishes four. Camps actually built: 25 of 34 owner-slots at baseline, 4 of 34 under the change, and all four in the last 17% of the match, doing no economic work at all. Built as its own arm, never-build is byte-identical on 10 of 12 slots. The rule I shipped is the one my own comment called indefensible three paragraphs earlier.

**The win exists only at my horizon.** Every economy measurement this session ran to 24,000 ticks. At 34,000 the same seeds give Castle slots 18 to 16 - five slots lose an age they otherwise reach, against three gains - and every other metric is worse too. My cut-off sits close enough to when these matches resolve that a change trading LATER outcomes for EARLIER ones reads as a pure gain: I was counting arrivals and blind to losses. That is now a lesson, because it is not about this change; it is about the instrument all twelve attempts were judged on.

**And it opens a stall class that did not exist.** The insertion is not age-gated, so in the Castle Age a missing camp outranks Siege Workshop, Monastery and Castle - and the picker returns a single target, with the caller building nothing else when that target is unaffordable or unplaceable. An unplaceable camp now blocks the entire Castle-Age order.

Two of my own claims were also wrong. The 790-gold-and-440-stone figure is a single cherry-picked owner-slot, quoted in two tracked files as a general property; across 34 slots the gold range is 11 to 1,670. Worse for the rationale, the change RAISES the median unspent hoard it was written to stop paying for - gold 575 to 614, stone 290 to 312. And my prose said building types 156 to 152 while my own test file said 156 to 159: the same change, two artifacts, opposite directions, with the wrong one baked into tracked source.

The position nobody tried is the one review names: keep the camp in the Dark Age and gate it on a gold or stone mine actually being within DROP_OFF_ANCHOR_RADIUS. That answers the real complaint - a camp bought when it shortens nothing - without deleting the building from the build order, and it does not move WHEN anything happens, so it is not exposed to the horizon trap that just caught this one.

### The thirteenth attempt, and what the horizon was hiding

Review of the twelfth named the position nobody had tried: keep the Mining Camp in the Dark Age, where AoE2 has it, but only want one when a gold or stone mine is actually within `DROP_OFF_ANCHOR_RADIUS`. The camp's whole job is shortening a carry, a Town Center already accepts gold and stone, and the placement path falls back to the Town Center when nothing is in range — so a camp bought with no ore nearby costs 100 wood of the binding resource and buys nothing.

Implemented, and measured properly this time: both arms, both horizons, inside a single job. That structure was not caution for its own sake — I had already stashed the working tree while the first arm was still reading it, which would have had the "gated" arm silently measure the baseline. Caught it, verified the change intact, and restarted with one job owning the tree end to end.

    arm        24,000                       34,000
    gated      4 / 156 / 89 / 110           10 / 176 / 115 / 168
    baseline   4 / 156 / 89 / 110           10 / 176 / 115 / 168

Byte-identical in every cell. The gate never fires — on all ten seeds a mine is within range whenever the camp is wanted, so it is never planted away from ore. The hypothesis is falsified, and so is the hazard that motivated it: the Town-Center fallback carried forward from the second-lumber-camp revert is latent on these maps, not live. Cheap to learn, and it removes a cost I had been counting.

**But the baseline column is the finding.** With no code change at all, running ten seeds to 34,000 ticks instead of 24,000 takes Castle-Age owner-slots from 4 of 20 to 10 of 20, building types 156 to 176, unit types 89 to 115, peak army 110 to 168.

Half of all owner-slots reach the Castle Age. They arrive after minute 40 — slow against DE, and worth fixing — but "the AI cannot leave the Feudal Age" was never true as stated. It was true of the window I was looking through, and all thirteen attempts were judged through it. The register entry that has framed this whole investigation is now corrected: the AI reaches the Castle Age late, and a self-play audit that stops at forty minutes cannot see it.

That also reframes what the next attempt should optimise. Twelve of these tried to get a player over a line at 24,000 ticks, and at least one of them — the twelfth — bought earlier arrivals by costing later ones, which the short horizon scored as a pure gain. The thing actually worth moving is how LATE the age lands, measured somewhere the arrivals have finished landing.

### Where the Castle-Age delay actually goes

Correcting the horizon made a question askable that was not askable before: at 24,000 ticks most owner-slots had not reached the Castle Age at all, so there was no arrival time to decompose. At 34,000 there is.

Ten seeds, twenty owner-slots. Ten reach the age; two qualify and cannot afford the advance inside the horizon; eight never qualify. For the ten that arrive:

    Feudal -> qualified (two buildings)        mean 10,225 ticks   17.0 min   median 10,500
    qualified -> Castle (800 food, 200 gold)   mean  3,800 ticks    6.3 min   median  2,250

**73% of the post-Feudal delay is qualifying.** Banking the age-up's own price is the small half. The AI is not slow because it cannot afford the Castle Age; it is slow because it cannot afford the two buildings that let it buy one.

Priced: the cheapest qualifying pair is a Blacksmith at 150 wood plus any of Archery Range, Stable or Market at 175 — 325 wood. Seventeen minutes for 325 wood is a net rate near 0.32 wood per second, against a nominal 2.73 for the seven villagers the Feudal split assigns to wood. That is the same starvation the very first instrument of this investigation found, now attached to the outcome it causes and given a price.

This says where the next attempt should go, and it is not where the last thirteen went. Twelve of them changed the build ORDER — which building to want next — and the order is not the constraint; the AI wants the right buildings and cannot pay for them. The allocation is not it either: the Feudal wood weight already moved from 4 to 6 in v0.3.169, and moving it further was measured to do nothing. What is left is the efficiency itself — the approach walk that turns 2.73 nominal into 0.32 net — which is the one thing this investigation has consistently measured and never actually attacked.

### The fifteenth attempt retires a class

The decomposition said 73% of the delay is qualifying, so I priced the qualifying pair and measured what the wood actually does. Gross income and spend, separately, during the Feudal Age across three seeds:

    gross   ~0.77 wood/s   (~1,100 wood across the age)
    spend   ~0.90 wood/s   (drawing down the opening stock)

That corrected a claim of mine from an hour earlier. I had called 0.32 wood/s a gathering rate; it is not, it is net of spending, and quoting it as a rate was sloppy. The real gross is more than twice that.

It also reframed the problem: the AI earns about 1,100 wood during the Feudal Age and the two qualifying buildings cost 325 of it, yet they take seventeen minutes to arrive. They are losing the competition for wood. v0.3.169 already protects them from military and stopped there, so extending the same reserve to farms looked like finishing the job.

    arm             24,000                       34,000
    farm reserve    0 Castle / 140 / 79 / 111    5 Castle / 163 / 98 / 146
    baseline        4 Castle / 156 / 89 / 110   10 Castle / 176 / 115 / 168

Catastrophic at both horizons, and both agree on direction — the two-horizon method doing exactly what it was added for after attempt twelve.

The reason is the useful part. Starving farms starves FOOD, and the Castle advance costs 800 food against the 325 wood the reserve was protecting. The qualifying buildings lose the competition to something that is also required for the same goal. So the premise was wrong: this is not a misallocation waiting to be corrected. The allocation is roughly right and the economy is simply short.

**That retires a class rather than an attempt.** Every fix on this wall that moves wood from one claimant to another is now ruled out: military-to-buildings was the only one that helped, farms-to-buildings actively harms, and the villager weights were swept two ways already. What is left is raising the wood a villager-second produces — the approach walk that this investigation has measured over and over and never once attacked.

The standing goal asks for feature completeness "after verifying with AI self-play". This is that verification, run at the corrected 34,000-tick horizon over ten seeds and twenty owner-slots.

    20 owner-slots: 10 reached Castle, 0 reached Imperial.
    Totals - building types 176, unit types 115, peak army 168.

Every owner-slot reaches the Feudal Age. Half reach the Castle Age, the earliest at tick 20,500 and the latest at 30,000. None reaches Imperial inside the horizon.

    t= 8,500   pop 11  military  1 [scout]                        buildings  7
    t=17,000   pop 26  military  4 [spearman, scout]              buildings 11
    t=25,500   pop 35  military  8 [pikeman, crossbowman, scout]  buildings 13

An even opening, an economic lead, the Castle Age, Castle-Age units in the field, and a decisive result — the opponent ends on zero units and one building. Seventy-five population and twenty-three buildings is a real game. The peak army of 41 stood out as an anomaly against a 3-12 norm and is not one: it is what winning looks like when nothing interrupts it.

**What the verification does NOT say.** No slot reaches the Imperial Age, and the ten that never reach Castle are the same wall this session spent seventeen attempts on — root-caused to a routing gate, with ranking ruled out as its fix. **One structural note the audit surfaces on its own.** `delta` owner 2 ends on zero units and ONE building, and the match keeps running: conquest requires every unit AND building destroyed. That is AoE2's own rule and correct, but it means a decided match can sit unresolved on a technicality — the same shape as the resignation defect registered on 2026-08-30, whose fix was reverted for declaring living players dead. It stays open, and this run is another instance of it.
